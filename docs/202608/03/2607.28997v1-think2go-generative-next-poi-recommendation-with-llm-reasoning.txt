## Abstract 

Next Point-of-Interest (POI) recommendation task focuses on min-ing user behavioral preference patterns from historical check-ins to provide personalized suggestions for the next destination. Exist-ing methods primarily rely on shallow contextual information and handcrafted feature interactions to predict the next POI. However, the inherent sparsity and complexity of user mobility patterns limit the computational capacity of non-reasoning models to capture deep intent, while large language models (LLMs) perform subopti-mally because they lack a deep understanding of semantic IDs (SIDs) when SIDs are trained separately. To address these limitations, we propose Think2Go , a novel generative next POI recommendation framework, which enhances the model’s comprehension of SID representations and explores diverse spatial-temporal patterns via test-time computational scaling. We unify supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL)-based reasoning within a single architecture, enabling joint optimization of memorization and adaptive reasoning to better retain user behavior patterns while exploring diverse user preferences. To further calibrate policy opti-mization in adaptive reasoning, we propose two advantage weight-ing mechanisms that integrate (1) prompt epistemic uncertainty ,estimated via kernel density methods to assess the spatial-temporal

## 1 Introduction 

Location-based social networks (LBSNs) are increasingly becoming an integral part of modern life, as a growing number of users share their check-in records at interesting points of interest (POIs) via mobile platforms such as Foursquare and Google Maps. As a result, POI recommendation has emerged as a key research task [ 36 , 41 ]based on LBSN data, aiming to understand users’ behavioral prefer-ences [ 48 , 62 ] from their historical trajectories and provide location-based services [ 15 , 19 , 61 ]. A notable variant of this task is next POI recommendation, which aims to predict the next location a user is likely to visit based on the spatial-temporal context of their check-in history and a given temporal cue [ 56 ], assisting users in trip planning [ 20 , 49 ] and in discovering locations of potential interest [39, 51, 60]. Recently, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated re-markable progress in contextual understanding [ 9], marking a sig-nificant breakthrough in tackling complex real-world tasks. This has motivated the use of LLM-based generative modeling as a novel paradigm for next POI recommendation, where the model directly generates the ID of the next POI at the target time based on user-provided prompts. Compared to traditional POI recommendation methods, this approach avoids the semantic loss that occurs when heterogeneous textual data are converted into numerical IDs. It effectively leverages the general knowledge of LLMs to capture contextual dependencies in user check-in trajectories. Moreover, semantic POI ID representations have been proposed to initialize IDs with similar meanings for geographically or functionally re-lated locations, further enhancing the LLM’s ability to understand and generalize POI semantics. Recent methods [ 17 , 18 , 22 , 26 ] based on LLMs primarily focus on fine-tuning pre-trained models but overlook the benefits of expand-ing computational capacity through test-time scaling, enhancing the next POI generation via reasoning. For instance, GNPR [ 42 ]fine-tunes the base model with pre-trained SIDs to enforce seman-tic alignment between the model and the ID space. However, this decoupled training strategy leads to an incomplete understanding of SID information, particularly under sparse POI recommenda-tion scenarios. As a result, current LLM-based approaches struggle to accurately incorporate SID signals during generation, yielding suboptimal performance. This insufficient comprehension of se-mantic IDs is further reflected in the model’s poor generalization on out-of-domain datasets and its limited capability to distinguish SID categories. These limitations highlight the importance of designing effective reasoning models to improve both SID understanding and sequen-tial modeling within LLMs. However, developing a generation-based POI recommendation framework with integrated reasoning is nontrivial due to several challenges: (1) Model Architecture: 

Traditional reasoning models rely on RL-based policy optimiza-tion, which assumes that the base model has sufficient predictive capability to sample correct answers. Moreover, overfitting dur-ing the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage can restrict exploration in subsequent RL training and decouple memorization from rea-soning, ultimately resulting in suboptimal learning [ 11 , 21 , 47 ]. (3) Model-Aware Adaptive Optimization: While Dr.GRPO [ 23 ] removes the variance term in advantage estimation to alleviate signal amplification caused by estimation bias, it still overlooks another form of bias: the tendency of the model to produce similar intra-group advantage distributions across samples of varying difficulty. For example, a hard instance with rewards [2.5, 2.5, 1.5] and an easy one with [4.5, 4.5, 3.5] both yield the same advantage [0.33, 0.33, –0.67] under mean-centered advantage estimation. This leads to uniform updates across tasks of different difficulty, which undermines fine-grained learning and limits the ability to assign appropriate update magnitudes based on model competence. To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose Think2Go as shown in Figure 1, a unified framework that combines SFT and RL-based reasoning to unlock LLM’s test-time reasoning capacity, thereby improving its comprehension of semantic identifiers and modeling of sequential user behaviors. To tackle the first challenge, we introduce a unified training paradigm that integrates both SFT and RL-based policy optimization. The model generates structured outputs in the form of Think->Go->Self-Correct->Answer , where the Self-Correct token serves as a bridge between the reasoning and SFT phase. The final Answer token is used for computing the SFT loss, while the Think and Go segments comprise the reasoning trajectory, which is optimized via policy gradients. This unified structure facilitates seamless learning between memorization and exploration. To address the second challenge, we propose an epis-temic uncertainty advantage calibration strategy for prompt-based learning. Specifically, we use kernel density estimation to assess the spatial-temporal similarity between the current query and the user’s historical behavior. This similarity quantifies epistemic un-certainty and adaptively modulates the advantage signal to better explore challenging instances. For the third challenge, we intro-duce difficulty-aware advantage weighting, which regularizes the reward of each sample by its maximum value to reflect prediction difficulty. This allows the model to adaptively adjust the scale of policy updates. As a result, the combined effect of prompt and model-aware calibrations serves as an implicit curriculum learn-ing mechanism, delivering fine-grained, instance-specific gradients that enable more effective and adaptive policy optimization. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: 

• We propose Think2Go, the first generative next POI rec-ommendation framework based on LLM reasoning, which unifies SFT and RL-based reasoning to better balance memo-rization and exploration. 

• We propose an advantage calibration mechanism that adapts to both prompt epistemic uncertainty and prediction diffi-culty, implicitly forming a competence-aware curriculum that refines exploration and enhances learning efficiency. 

• Think2Go consistently outperforms baselines across three public datasets. Further evaluation on out-of-domain datasets and SID classification tasks shows that reasoning improves LLMs’ understanding of SIDs and enhances generalization. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Next POI Recommendation 

Existing literature on next POI recommendation primarily focuses on modeling users’ historical check-in sequences to capture the spatial-temporal transition patterns [ 33 , 38 , 46 ] between POIs, mak-ing it a typical sequential recommendation task [ 12 , 13 , 35 , 57 , 63 ]. Early studies [ 1, 6, 30 ] widely adopted Markov Chain (MC) meth-ods [ 3], leveraging personalized transition matrices to model user preferences and mobility patterns. However, these approaches fall short in capturing users’ complex interests and implicit behavioral signals compared to deep learning-based models. With the rise of deep learning, research has gradually shifted towards models based on Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) [ 10 ]and attention mechanisms [ 40 ]. For instance, DeepMove [ 5] in-tegrates attention and gating mechanisms to jointly learn users’ long-term periodic and short-term preferences. STGN [ 59 ] enhances LSTM architectures by incorporating spatial-temporal gates into the gating units, enabling the modeling of long- and short-term spatial-temporal preferences. STAN [ 28 ] and STiSAN [ 43 ] further introduce attention mechanisms to capture both continuous and non-continuous spatial-temporal contextual intervals in the mod-eling process. In addition, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have been leveraged to model high-level POI transition patterns by uti-lizing the user-POI graph structure [ 29 , 32 ]. For example, Graph-Flashback [ 34 ] constructs a powerful weighted user-POI knowledge graph to initialize POI embeddings, while STHGCN [ 50 ] employs hypergraphs to capture trajectory-level collaborative signals. Recently, LLMs with extensive commonsense knowledge have shown strong potential in recommendation tasks, leading to a rise in LLM-based approaches for next POI recommendation [ 24 ]. LLM-Mob [ 45 ] enhances the modeling of historical trajectory con-text through in-context learning. NextLocLLM [ 22 ] attempts to predict spatial coordinates directly to improve spatial reasoning within LLMs. LLM4POI [ 17 ] reformulates next POI recommenda-tion as a question-answering task and fine-tunes the model us-ing QLoRA-based supervised learning. Refine-POI [ 16 ] applies Group-based Rule-guided Policy Optimization (GRPO) [ 37 ] with recommendation-driven reward design to generate top-k recom-mendation lists. However, they have not explored reasoning-enhanced models to boost generalization. While Refine-POI leverages the GRPO framework for reasoning-based prediction, it overlooks the optimization of the advantage function, which limits performance improvements. 

## 2.2 Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning 

Recent advancements in generative Artificial Intelligence (AI), par-ticularly with models like OpenAI-o1 [ 31 ] and Deepseek-R1 [ 14 ], have extended the scope of research from large language models (LLMs) to large reasoning models (LRMs). This shift is predicated on the application of the Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) training framework, coupled with test-time scal-ing and an emphasis on deep thinking capabilities. The core inno-vation lies in explicitly leveraging a long chain-of-thought (CoT) mechanism [ 25 ]. This encourages models to generate a substantial number of intermediate reasoning tokens before arriving at a final answer, leading to significant performance improvements in com-plex domains such as mathematics, code generation, and question answering. GRPO has emerged as a leading reinforcement learning algo-rithm for training reasoning-augmented large language models GRPO eliminates the need for a separate value model by employing group-level rewards based on rule validation, thereby enabling self-play validation to enhance reasoning efficacy [ 4]. However, GRPO faces challenges such as inefficient sampling and length bias, which have spurred further optimizations in algorithms like Decoupled Advantage Policy Optimization (DAPO) [ 55 ] and Dr.GRPO [ 23 ]. Large Reasoning Models have garnered considerable interest in the domain of generative recommendation systems. R 2EC [ 54 ] re-conceptualizes recommendation model architectures by integrat-ing an inherent reasoning capability within a regression process. R2Rec [ 58 ] employs sampled interaction chains from user-item graphs to guide chain-of-thought reasoning. Nevertheless, both overlook sparse spatiotemporal behavior patterns, limiting their policy optimization and overall performance.

## 4 Methods 

Think2Go is a large next POI recommendation model that adopts a “think-then-recommend” paradigm. Section 4.1 presents the ar-chitecture, wherein generative reasoning and recommendation are jointly integrated into a unified framework. Section 4.2 introduces a tailored RL algorithm designed to alleviate entropy collapse and maintain an effective balance between exploration and exploitation. 

## 4.1 Model Architecture 

Our proposed Think2Go model is built upon a decoder-only back-bone, leveraging self-correction prompts as a bridge to unify super-vised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL). In exist-ing RLHF approaches, they typically perform SFT followed by RL, which limits learning efficiency and leads to catastrophic forgetting from SFT[ 11 , 21 ]. To overcome this, we unify SFT and RL within a single architecture. It employs SFT to ensure the model retains correct answers, while RL is used to enhance the model’s ability to explore and infer correct answers through reasoning. As illustrated in Figure 2, the old policy model first generates a reasoning trace composed of a Think step and a final prediction (Go). While the correct answer is typically appended for SFT, we instead introduce a self-correct prompt to provide contextual grounding. This forms a Think–>Go–>Self–correct–>Answer structure, where tokens from the reasoning trace are used for RL, and tokens from the appended answer are used for SFT. 4.2.2 Rule-based Reward Estimation. Given the sampled trajecto-ries, the next step is to assign appropriate rewards to each one. Con-ventionally, reward signals include format rewards and evaluation-based rewards (e.g., Acc@1), which encourage the model to generate outputs with correct answer formatting and ultimately improve task performance. However, the inherent sparsity of such rewards severely restricts effective optimization. This is particularly prob-lematic in the initial training phase, where the model often fails to generate outputs in the prescribed format and much less the correct answer. To address this challenge, we design a progressive reward mech-anism that first encourages the model to generate well-formatted responses and then to produce semantically correct answers. For format rewards, we use a two-level scheme: if the output contains a partial format (i.e., <answer>: ), a reward of 0.5 is assigned; if the out-put satisfies the full structure </think><answer>:SID</answer> 

and exceeds a length threshold of 300 tokens, a full reward of 1.0 is given. Benefiting from SFT, the model gradually learns to produce partially correct formats before mastering the complete structure. The length constraint is crucial for preventing reward hacking, where the model exploits formatting alone to obtain undeserved rewards. For evaluation-based rewards, we adopt a fine-grained scoring strategy based on decomposed semantic ID units. Each correctly predicted unit receives a reward of 3 

> 𝐿

, where 𝐿 denotes the total number of semantic units. This approach provides graded feedback for partially correct answers and encourages the model to incrementally refine its output toward complete correctness. 

4.2.3 Spatial-Temporal Epistemic Uncertainty (STEU). Group-wise normalized advantage estimation captures the model’s relative com-petence on given prompts and serves as the basis for subsequent KDD ’26, August 09–13, 2026, Jeju Island, Republic of Korea Zhuang Zhuang et al. 

policy updates. However, this uniform treatment fails to account for the varying difficulty of different instances, resulting in equally weighted updates regardless of prediction complexity. Such insen-sitivity may diminish learning incentives for challenging samples, thereby constraining the model’s exploration capacity and general-ization ability. 4.2.4 Difficulty-aware Reward Gap (DRG). Dr.GRPO removes the regularization term to avoid difficulty bias caused by excessively small group-wise standard deviations when the input prompts are either too hard or too easy, and retains only the centered outcome reward as the advantage. However, this leads to a problem in multi-reward settings where predictions of varying quality receive iden-tical advantages if their rewards follow similar group-wise distri-butions. As a result, it weakens the model’s ability to distinguish between better and worse strategies, reduces the directional speci-ficity of gradient updates, and increases the risk of converging to suboptimal local minima. For example, consider a group size of 3, where the reward values are [4.5, 4.5, 3.5] for an easy instance and [2.5, 2.5, 1.5] for a hard one. After mean-centering, both groups yield the same advantage distribution: [0.33, 0.33, -0.67]. This identical advantage assignment leads the model to perform equivalent policy updates for both groups, disregarding the absolute magnitude of the rewards and failing to capture the intrinsic difficulty of the underlying prediction task. As a result, the optimization process becomes insensitive to task difficulty, weakening the learning incentives for challenging samples and ultimately constraining the effectiveness of policy optimization and the upper bound of model performance. To mitigate the value dilution introduced by intra-group normal-ization, we re-scale the group-wise advantage using the original reward magnitude relative to the maximum reward to preserve the signal of absolute performance. This adjustment mitigates the loss of reward intensity information caused by standardization alone. Our DRG is defined as:

, (9) where 𝑅 denotes the current sample reward, 𝑅 𝑚𝑎𝑥 denotes the max-imum reward value. Serving as scaling factors based on the centered advantage, these coefficients enable the dif-ferentiation of instance difficulty, leading to more fine-grained and adaptive policy optimization. Through comparative experiments, we further observe that it effectively alleviates entropy collapse by maintaining a high entropy level for the reasoning tokens, which implies greater exploration capacity.

## 5.6 Analysis on Advantage Estimation 

Accurate advantage estimation is critical for mitigating entropy collapse and finding globally optimal solutions in policy gradient RL. To assess this, we compare our method with Refine-POI, focusing on the behavior of their advantage estimators during training in the Figure 3. We report entropy, Acc@1, and reward over training steps, and summarize the following findings: (1) Entropy stability: Think2Go better mitigates entropy collapse than Refine-POI, enabling sus-tained exploration. This improvement stems from the DRG, which adaptively scales the advantage based on reward signals—assigning smaller updates to incorrect predictions and maintaining higher entropy levels. We also observe that entropy first drops sharply, then rises and stabilizes. This is due to the base model initially lack-ing knowledge of semantic IDs and answer templates; once such knowledge is acquired, entropy increases as the model explores more diverse outputs. (2) Accuracy and reward performance: 

Think2Go consistently outperforms Refine-POI in both Acc@1 and reward throughout training, demonstrating that its advantage esti-mator facilitates more effective policy optimization. (3) Template generalization: The consistently high reward (above 1.5) after around 400 training steps suggests that the model has effectively learned to produce structurally correct responses. This is largely attributed to the supervision from the SFT loss, which enforces template-conforming output.

## 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we study a novel generative next POI recommendation framework with reasoning, aiming to improve model generalization by increasing the computational depth of inference. We introduce a unified fine-tuning and reinforcement learning–based reasoning model to enhance the model’s reasoning ability, where the policy is optimized by calibrating the advantage based on both the epis-temic uncertainty of the data and the difficulty agnostic bias in the model’s advantage estimation. Building upon this design, we propose Think2Go, an advantage-calibrated reasoning model for time-sensitive next POI recommendation. Think2Go employs kernel density estimation to quantify epistemic uncertainty by measur-ing the spatial-temporal proximity between the current query and the user’s historical activity patterns. Moreover, difficulty-aware calibration enables reward-sensitive advantage estimation by scal-ing rewards relative to the maximum, thus mitigating bias from uniform update strength. This allows for more fine-grained and adaptive control over the policy updates, ensuring that samples of varying difficulty contribute appropriately to the learning process. Extensive experiments and ablation studies validate the effective-ness of our proposed CAPO method, demonstrating its superior generalization capability.

## A Implementation Detail 

For the fine-tuning module, we utilized LLaMA3.1-8B as the base model, fine-tuning it with LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) using a rank of 8, an alpha of 16, and a dropout of 0.1. The maximum generation length for tokens was set to 2048, with a batch size of 1 per GPU. The learning rate was configured to 2e-5, incorporating a 20-step warm-up phase. For generating the reasoning module, we used VLLM for the inference module, setting its maximum generation length to 1024, a temperature of 1.3, and returning 4 rollout samples with a beam search width of 10. Our hardware setup comprised five Nvidia L40s GPUs. Four of these GPUs were dedicated to data training, while the remaining one was equipped with the VLLM model to generate online sampling data. Each input prompt consisted of the user’s 50 most recent check-in records. During inference, we employ beam search with a size of 15 to generate multiple candidate predictions.

## E Analysis on Model Efficiency 

In this paper, we propose Think2Go, which unifies SFT and RL-based reasoning models. We utilize semantic IDs as data and only incorporate temporal information to describe check-in trajectories. Compared to conventional RL-based reasoning methods, this ap-proach achieves higher efficiency. Table 10 reports a comparison of the efficiency between our method and Refine-POI (RL methods based on standard text prompts). Both methods were trained on the NYC dataset. It can be observed that our method achieves higher efficiency in both training and testing time and reduces memory consumption. This improvement benefits from using semantic IDs as POI identifiers, which reduces redundant input information, and from online sampling with VLLM during the reasoning stage, which also helps accelerate sampling speed.
